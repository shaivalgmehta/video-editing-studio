# Podcast Repurposing — Editorial Skill

## Purpose

Transform substantial podcast conversations into platform-native pieces of content that build **discovery, utility, credibility, familiarity, and long-term authority**. Do not merely chop up recordings, publish more posts, or promote the episode in every format.

**North star:** Each repurposed asset should work for someone who has never heard of the podcast, deliver genuine value, and give the audience a reason to trust or remember the host.

**Primary jobs by format** (not exclusive):

| Format | Primary job |
| --- | --- |
| Teaser | Build anticipation for a forthcoming episode |
| Reels / Shorts | Get discovered by new audiences |
| Carousels | Make ideas useful, saveable, shareable |
| Quote graphics | Make one idea memorable and shareable |
| LinkedIn posts | Demonstrate the host's judgment, interpretation and expertise |
| Newsletters | Deepen relationships and create repeat attention |

### Source case study: Steven Bartlett × Rick Rubin

Examples supplied from *The Diary of a CEO* episode include: a pre-release cinematic teaser with the caption “Sitting down with a true music legend tomorrow”; Reels labelled “Kanye Is Totally Fearless.”, “The Secret Behind Jay-Z's Process.”, “What Makes You Great?”, “You Were Born Creative.”, and “Jay-Z Never Did This.”; the carousel “6 Life Lessons from Rick Rubin”; and a LinkedIn post beginning “He knows what your favourite artists don't tell you.” that discusses Steven waiting eight years to interview Rick.

These examples illustrate editorial approaches; **do not claim any format, edit, or posting schedule caused specific view counts** without evidence. A newsletter for this episode was proposed as a strategy example, **not** supplied as a verified Bartlett publication.

## Global editorial rules

1. Find the strongest **ideas, tension, stories, and meaningful payoffs** in the episode before choosing any format.
2. Give each format a distinct editorial job, audience, angle, and consumption experience.
3. Value source material and accuracy over quantity. Do not fabricate claims, statistics, engagement counts, guest quotations, or attributed insights.
4. **Exact quotes must be verbatim.** If condensed or rewritten, label them as paraphrases, not quotes; do not change their meaning.
5. Choose the angle and central insight first; write the hook second. Deliver the promised payoff.
6. Make assets understandable without prior familiarity with the host, guest, or episode.
7. Preserve the host's *point of view*, not just the guest's celebrity and ideas.
8. Use clean mobile-first presentation, clear type, purposeful imagery and editing, and restrained branding. Avoid decorative clutter.
9. Spread distribution across release stages: anticipation, launch, post-release discovery, and ongoing value.
10. Track platform-specific signals and business outcomes. Reach alone does not prove authority or podcast audience growth.

## 1. Podcast teaser

**Principle: Reveal the promise. Protect the payoff.**

A strong teaser gives viewers a compelling taste of the episode as a whole—subject, stakes, range of fascinating ideas, emotional experience, and reason to watch—**without giving away the complete answers or resolution**. Think movie trailer, not shortened episode recap. Do not cram in literally every topic; select the most intriguing dimensions.

### Checklist

- [ ] Lead with a recognisable entry point: guest, achievement, conflict, subject or striking moment.
- [ ] Establish why the conversation matters to the intended audience.
- [ ] Introduce a **specific curiosity gap**, not empty vagueness.
- [ ] Show the scope, emotional tone and promise of the conversation without giving away the key explanations.
- [ ] Shape a miniature narrative: setup → tension → unresolved question.
- [ ] Select connected moments, rather than a random compilation of exciting clips.
- [ ] Demonstrate guest significance with relevant evidence or properly licensed archival imagery.
- [ ] Open strongly: omit slow introductions and logos before the hook.
- [ ] Use purposeful pacing, pauses, shot changes, sound design and music; avoid effects for their own sake.
- [ ] Keep dialogue audible and subtitles accurate, readable, and within safe margins.
- [ ] Use visual variety (interview shots, expressive close-ups, relevant supporting footage).
- [ ] State release date/time and one clear next action (follow, subscribe, set a reminder or return).
- [ ] Make the full episode easy to find on launch.
- [ ] Ensure the teaser represents the guest and episode accurately—no misleading drama.
- [ ] Test it with a stranger: do they know why to watch and still want to discover the answers?

**Case study:** The Rick Rubin teaser's dark cinematic visual language, guest legacy, unfinished glimpses and “tomorrow” caption create a sense of event. Its exact release information turns intrigue into a reason to return.

**Suggested experiment:** A 30–60-second teaser with first 3 seconds = compelling promise, next 10 = guest relevance, middle = connected tensions, ending = open question and release information. Test posting roughly 24–48 hours before release, plus a launch reminder. These are editorial starting points, not established rules from Bartlett.

**Don't:** Tell the entire story, string together unrelated loud moments, reveal every lesson, or produce a generic guest-credential montage.

## 2. Instagram Reels / YouTube Shorts

**Principle: One clip, one complete, memorable insight for someone who doesn't know your podcast.**

### Checklist

- [ ] One central thought viewers can summarise in a sentence.
- [ ] Specific hook: surprising claim, recognised subject, consequential mistake, unanswered question, useful discovery or meaningful contradiction.
- [ ] Authentic curiosity gap with a worthwhile promised payoff.
- [ ] Immediate enough context to understand the claim, without introducing the show at length.
- [ ] Evidence, explanation, example, reveal or story supporting the headline.
- [ ] Satisfying payoff delivered within the clip; no bait-and-switch.
- [ ] Clean ending without unrelated trailing conversation.
- [ ] Remove dead space and tangents without changing meaning or emotional texture.
- [ ] Readable, accurately synced captions and purposeful speaker framing.
- [ ] Native vertical composition with faces and text inside interface-safe areas.
- [ ] Angle matches a recognisable interest, problem or aspiration of the target audience.
- [ ] Each clip from the episode offers a *different* reason to care.

**Five effective hook patterns from supplied Rick Rubin assets:**

| Actual supplied headline | Attention mechanism |
| --- | --- |
| “Kanye Is Totally Fearless.” | Bold character claim about someone recognised |
| “The Secret Behind Jay-Z's Process.” | Access to a hidden method |
| “What Makes You Great?” | Personally relevant question |
| “You Were Born Creative.” | Belief about viewer identity |
| “Jay-Z Never Did This.” | Contradiction / missing explanation |

**Clip structure:** hook → minimum essential context → development/evidence → payoff → optional bridge to full episode.

**Suggested cadence:** Test 4–6 **distinct** Reels for a substantial weekly interview, spread across pre-release, launch day and the next 7–14 days. This is not a verified Bartlett cadence.

**Measure:** non-follower reach, 3-second hold/retention, average watch time, shares, profile visits, episode visits and subscribers where attributable.

**Don't:** Make six nearly identical cuts with sensational rewritten titles, bury the payoff in irrelevant context, or mistake views for lasting audience growth.

## 3. Carousels

**Principle: Make a mini-resource people want to save, share or revisit.**

### Checklist

- [ ] Cover promises a precise, meaningful outcome (not generic “episode highlights”).
- [ ] Slides belong to one connected theme/framework/list of lessons.
- [ ] Material is useful enough to revisit in a month.
- [ ] One complete insight per slide.
- [ ] Each slide works without listening to the episode.
- [ ] Deliberate progression, not arbitrary ordering.
- [ ] Include explanation or examples where a naked quote would be too thin.
- [ ] Clear, accessible type, generous space, strong hierarchy.
- [ ] Consistent visual treatment and accurate source attribution.
- [ ] Finish with a *natural* save/share prompt tied to the delivered value.

**Case study:** “6 Life Lessons from Rick Rubin” packages distinct, coherent life ideas rather than summarising the interview. Examples from the provided carousel include “Happiness isn't the goal. Peace is.”; “Risk doesn't threaten greatness. It reveals it.”; and “Every label you accept makes your world smaller.” Its closing slide asks viewers to send the lessons to someone who needs them.

**Suggested sequence:** promise/cover → one insight per slide → final related share/save invitation. One substantive carousel per strong episode is a reasonable test, not a mandate.

**Measure:** saves and shares relative to reach, profile visits, progression/slides viewed where available.

**Don't:** Force every interview into a generic list, make slides dependent on a transcript, or design decorative but substance-free quote stacks.

## 4. LinkedIn posts — host-led authority through storytelling

**Core principle:** Do not promote the episode first. Make the guest, the conversation, **and the host's curiosity and judgment** interesting. A strong LinkedIn post explains *why the host wanted the conversation, what surprised them, and why the reader should care*. The post stands alone as worthwhile writing; the episode is a natural next step.

### Evidence base: seven supplied Steven Bartlett LinkedIn posts

The user supplied screenshot examples from *The Diary of a CEO* about Eric Weinstein, Dana White, Shawn Ryan, Adam Neumann, Druski, Rick Rubin and Professor Jiang Xueqin. The following are **editorial observations of those screenshots**, not validated performance or causal explanations for engagement.

| Guest | Opening / hook | Central curiosity / deeper story |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Eric Weinstein | “Is the 80-year world order starting to fall apart?” | What dangers and structural changes are most people missing? |
| Dana White | “The UFC was bought for $2 million.” followed by a claimed $50 billion value | Why keep working relentlessly after tremendous financial success? |
| Shawn Ryan | “He infiltrated criminal networks in Colombia because normal life was boring.” | How have extreme experiences changed the way he sees life and faith? |
| Adam Neumann | “13,000 employees became 3 in the space of one week.” | What happens to someone's self-belief when success collapses? |
| Druski | “He's never spoken about this side of his life.” | What personal experiences shaped the ability to read people and create comedy? |
| Rick Rubin | Steven says he waited eight years for the interview | How does a producer remain creatively valuable across artists and generations? |
| Professor Jiang Xueqin | “The world is collapsing.” (attributed to the guest) | What systems and historical forces explain geopolitical changes? |

Quotes and numeric details above reproduce **claims or statements in the supplied posts**. Verify independently before asserting them as established facts in new writing.

### Nine editorial techniques to apply

1. **Lead with the story, not the episode announcement.** Start with a surprising fact, dramatic contrast, unresolved question or consequential decision. Guest name can come later. The opening must create a *specific* question a reader wants answered.
2. **Establish credibility through particular details.** Prefer concrete achievements, scale, time invested, before/after contrast or firsthand experience to “incredible guest.” Every fact should support the story, not serve as random decoration.
3. **Reveal the host's driving question.** Why was this person worth interviewing? What did the host genuinely need or want to understand? This turns the interview into an investigation rather than a guest biography.
4. **Create a second curiosity gap.** After introducing the guest, shift into a deeper contradiction, surprising exchange or unresolved personal question. The story should progress rather than spending all intrigue in the first line.
5. **Move from public reputation to a less-known private dimension.** Fame → underlying mindset; business success → personal motivations; entertaining persona → formative experience. Avoid inventing hidden details not supported by the interview.
6. **Show the host's reaction with observable evidence.** Describe what surprised, challenged or changed the host's mind, and the specific exchange or behaviour that prompted it. “This person is thoughtful” is weak; showing how the guest noticed a change in expression and asked about it is strong.
7. **Write topic bullets as mini-hooks.** Each item should offer a different curiosity mechanism: dramatic decision, debated belief, personal event, famous relationship or unresolved question. “Leadership” is weak; “the leadership mistake that cost the founder their best employees” is stronger *if accurate*.
8. **Make room for disagreement.** When guests are controversial, separate curiosity about their thinking from endorsement. An invitation to learn from a worldview does not validate its claims. Check factual assertions and preserve context.
9. **Close on reader value, not click pressure.** Return to what the audience might learn, think about or understand differently. Add a concise episode availability note only after earning their attention.

### Repeatable writing architecture (adapt, do not copy mechanically)

1. **The hook:** the most interesting question, fact, contradiction or transformation.
2. **The character:** name the guest; give 1–3 precise, relevant credibility markers.
3. **The pursuit:** explain why the host wanted this particular conversation.
4. **The turn:** reveal a surprising observation or contradiction, with a concrete example.
5. **The menu:** offer 3–6 distinct, curiosity-driven discussion points; each a compelling reason to watch, not a generic category.
6. **The reflection:** what stayed with the host, challenged their beliefs or deepened their understanding?
7. **The meaning:** connect the conversation to a larger relevant business, professional or human question.
8. **The invitation:** finish with a natural, brief link/availability mention where appropriate.

**This is an editorial logic, not a mandatory eight-paragraph template.** Sequence, length and number of bullets should respond to the actual story. Do not mechanically manufacture reactions, revelations or praise.

### Hook quality: distinguish topic from intrigue

- Weak: “Great interview with a successful founder about leadership.”
- Stronger: “13,000 employees became 3 in one week.” (From the supplied Adam Neumann post; factual context must be checked when reused.)
- Weak: “We discussed creativity with Rick Rubin.”
- Stronger: “How does one person keep helping extraordinary artists across different generations create their best work?” (Interpretation of the question in Steven's supplied post.)

A strong hook generally combines **a clear subject, interesting tension and a worthwhile payoff**. Specificity is more useful than sensational phrasing. Never exaggerate beyond the source conversation.

### Extraction brief — complete before drafting

- [ ] **Most interesting fact:** What is the surprising, consequential or unexpected reality in the episode? Where is it supported?
- [ ] **Guest credibility:** Which specific experiences or achievements make their perspective valuable? Verify figures and names.
- [ ] **Host's curiosity:** Why did this host *personally* want this guest? What question did they bring to the recording?
- [ ] **Unexpected discovery:** What moment, exchange or observation surprised the host or altered their thinking? Is that genuinely supported?
- [ ] **Five mini-hooks:** Extract several distinct topics, each with a specific tension and promised insight, without revealing every resolution.
- [ ] **Deeper meaning:** What larger decision, problem or human question connects this conversation to the intended audience?
- [ ] **Reason to watch:** Who specifically benefits, and why should this episode earn their time?

### Final LinkedIn quality checklist

- [ ] First line works independently of the guest's fame or podcast brand.
- [ ] Opening introduces a *particular* tension, not vague superlatives.
- [ ] Guest background uses only the most relevant, verified details.
- [ ] The host's reason for the conversation is genuine and distinctive.
- [ ] At least one specific moment substantiates a claimed surprise or insight.
- [ ] Reader gets a coherent story rather than a string of disconnected highlights.
- [ ] Discussion bullets are specific mini-promises with different entry points.
- [ ] Avoid blanket endorsement of unverified or controversial claims.
- [ ] Voice is conversational; concrete facts precede abstract interpretation; paragraphs remain readable.
- [ ] Post gives meaningful value even without clicking through.
- [ ] Ending explains why the conversation matters, rather than begging for engagement.
- [ ] Above all, the reader remembers **why the host is worth following**, not just the guest.

### Platform usage, cadence, and measurement

**Why LinkedIn:** It allows nuanced first-person professional reflection, extended stories, and discussion about business, leadership, work and judgment. Adapt angles to the professional reader; do not paste a generic episode description.

**Suggested cadence:** One substantive host-perspective post for each major interview; add one or two only when they develop genuinely distinct ideas. This is a recommended test, **not** a documented Bartlett schedule.

**Measure:** quality and relevance of comments, saves, qualified profile visits, links/clicks where trackable, inbound conversations and business-relevant follow-through. The supplied screenshots do not provide consistent evidence to attribute specific performance to the writing formula.

**Do not:** Invent host opinions, turn purported quotes into fabricated dialogue, publish unverified statistics as facts, treat guest access as equivalent to endorsement, or write every post in precisely the same structure.

## 5. Newsletters

**Principle: Take one interesting podcast idea and make it more useful than it was in the conversation.**

### Checklist

- [ ] One specific insight with enough substance to warrant an email.
- [ ] Subject line offers a precise benefit, question or tension.
- [ ] First paragraph starts with a problem, observation or story—not episode housekeeping.
- [ ] Coherent argument: premise → explanation → useful conclusion.
- [ ] Add genuine host interpretation, context, examples or application.
- [ ] Accurately attribute guest statements; distinguish quote from interpretation.
- [ ] Readable in a few minutes with natural paragraphs and subheadings where needed.
- [ ] One worthwhile reader action, experiment or change of thinking.
- [ ] Delivers value without requiring a click.
- [ ] Links naturally to the relevant moment in the full episode.
- [ ] Consistent editorial promise so subscribing makes sense.
- [ ] Invite a thoughtful response when relevant.

**Suggested Rick Rubin email (not a verified published asset):** “Why creativity gets harder when you start trying to impress everyone.” Explore a single idea, interpret it for founders/creatives, add one practical application, and point to the episode for depth.

**Basic structure:** compelling opening → what the guest said/experienced → deeper lesson/host interpretation → practical application → source-episode link.

**Suggested cadence:** One focused newsletter for each major episode, around release or 24–72 hours afterwards as an experiment.

**Measure:** meaningful clicks, replies, retention/unsubscribes, qualified episode traffic. Open rate is noisy because of email privacy protections.

- Mel Robbins: personal host voice and relationship, not just episode promotion.

## 6. Quote graphics

**Principle: A strong quote makes someone stop, think, and feel “that's exactly right” or “I've never thought of it that way.”**

### Checklist

- [ ] One complete, understandable standalone thought.
- [ ] An unusual perspective, overlooked truth, surprising reframing or useful observation.
- [ ] Specific wording, rather than interchangeable clichés.
- [ ] Emotional or intellectual response: recognition, curiosity, inspiration, challenge, amusement.
- [ ] Memorable contrast, analogy, imagery or unexpected conclusion.
- [ ] Natural reason to save or send to someone.
- [ ] Brief without losing essential context.
- [ ] **Quote exactly**, or label a rewrite as paraphrase; do not fabricate.
- [ ] Quote is the clear visual focus, with generous space and legible contrast.
- [ ] Relevant imagery supports meaning; portrait is optional, not mandatory.
- [ ] Subtle but clear speaker/show attribution.
- [ ] Recognisable, consistent brand typography and colour.
- [ ] Mobile-feed readability and safe margins.

**Five extraction angles:** contrarian statement; reframing; relatable personal truth; unexpected lesson; memorable analogy.

**Examples from supplied Rick Rubin carousel:** “Happiness isn't the goal. Peace is.” (contrarian); “Risk doesn't threaten greatness. It reveals it.” (reframing); “Every label you accept makes your world smaller.” (personal truth). Check transcript before treating any redesigned form as verbatim audio dialogue.

**Design rules:** Emphasise *meaningful* words rather than arbitrarily colouring random parts of the sentence; don't overlay copy on busy faces; avoid icon overload and multiple calls to action; use strong portrait when it genuinely contributes, or typography-only when stronger.

**Suggested cadence:** Test 2–4 genuinely strong quote graphics per substantial episode, spaced over one or two weeks; post only one if that's all the episode merits.

**Measure:** saves, shares, relevant comments, profile visits, qualified new followers.

**Example contrast (illustrative, not attributed):**
- Weak: “We always worked really hard to make our customers happy.”
- Strong: “We didn't have a customer acquisition problem. We had a reason-to-come-back problem.”

The stronger version reframes a business diagnosis and can also generate a Reel, carousel, LinkedIn post or deeper email.

## Packaging workflow

1. **Watch/review source episode:** note timestamps, claims, examples, narrative context, guest names, and quote accuracy.
2. **Build an insight inventory:** for each candidate capture core insight, audience, tension, proof/payoff, source timestamp, and host interpretation.
3. **Prioritise:** select moments that are distinctive, useful, accurate and relevant. Reject generic, contextless, or misleading excerpts.
4. **Map to formats:** teaser = unresolved promise; Reel = single thought; carousel = grouped useful lessons; quote = highly memorable line; LinkedIn = host interpretation; newsletter = deep application.
5. **Write/editorial review:** match platform's behaviour and audience without changing truth.
6. **Design/edit:** purposeful pace, strong hierarchy, restrained branding, clear citations and text, mobile-safe framing.
7. **Quality check:** use the format checklist above; verify quotes, names, factual claims, and licensing for reused footage/images.
8. **Distribute consistently:** plan before release / launch / after release, not all at once.
9. **Measure and learn:** platform-specific indicators plus actual episode and business outcomes. Refine the next episode's angle selection.

## What not to expect

Repurposing **does not automatically** create viral reach, increased YouTube views, subscribers or authority. A weak source interview cannot be rescued by generating twenty interchangeable clips. Sustainable results depend on meaningful ideas, editorial taste, packaging, consistency, distribution, audience fit, and learning from results. Do not claim that social views directly caused full-episode views without attribution evidence.

## Final cross-format five-question test

1. Would this still be interesting without the podcast attached?
2. Does it deliver value instead of merely advertising the episode?
3. Is it adapted to how people consume this platform?
4. Does it strengthen the reputation of the **host**, not only the guest?
5. Would someone who enjoyed it have a good reason to follow us for more?

**Short summary:** Teasers make people anticipate. Reels make people discover. Carousels make ideas useful and saveable. Quote graphics make ideas memorable. LinkedIn demonstrates judgment. Newsletters build an ongoing relationship. The podcast itself offers the full depth of the conversation.
